<h2>Injection Routes</h2>

<p>
  Injection routes refer to the various ways medications are administered using a syringe and needle. The main routes include:
</p>

<h3>1. Intramuscular (IM) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Definition:</strong> Administration of medication directly into the muscle.</li>
  <li><strong>Common Sites:</strong> Deltoid, Vastus lateralis, Gluteus maximus.</li>
  <li><strong>Angle of Insertion:</strong> 90 degrees.</li>
  <li><strong>Example:</strong> Vaccines (e.g., Hepatitis B, Influenza), Painkillers (Diclofenac).</li>
  <li><strong>Advantages:</strong> Rapid absorption, prolonged effect.</li>
  <li><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Painful, risk of nerve injury.</li>
</ul>

<h3>2. Intravenous (IV) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Definition:</strong> Injection directly into the bloodstream via a vein.</li>
  <li><strong>Common Sites:</strong> Cephalic vein, Basilic vein, Median cubital vein.</li>
  <li><strong>Angle of Insertion:</strong> 25 degrees.</li>
  <li><strong>Example:</strong> Antibiotics (Ceftriaxone), Chemotherapy drugs.</li>
  <li><strong>Advantages:</strong> Immediate effect, accurate dosage.</li>
  <li><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Risk of phlebitis, infection.</li>
</ul>

<h3>3. Subcutaneous (SC) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Definition:</strong> Injection into the subcutaneous tissue (fat layer beneath the skin).</li>
  <li><strong>Common Sites:</strong> Abdomen, Upper arm, Thigh.</li>
  <li><strong>Angle of Insertion:</strong> 45 degrees.</li>
  <li><strong>Example:</strong> Insulin, Heparin.</li>
  <li><strong>Advantages:</strong> Slow and steady absorption.</li>
  <li><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Limited volume of drug administration.</li>
</ul>

<h3>4. Intradermal (ID) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into the dermis (just below the outer layer of the skin)</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Localized effect or diagnostic testing</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Tuberculosis (Mantoux test), allergy testing</li>
  <li><b>Common Sites</b>: Forearm, upper back</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Tuberculin test, Xylocaine Sensitivity Test</li>
</ul>

<h3>5. Intra-articular Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into a joint space</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Direct treatment for joint-related conditions</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Arthritis, pain relief</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Corticosteroid injection in the knee joint</li>
</ul>

<h3>6. Intrathecal Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) via the spinal canal</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Direct action on the central nervous system (CNS)</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Spinal anesthesia, chemotherapy, pain management</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Epidural anesthesia during childbirth</li>
</ul>

<h3>7. Intraosseous (IO) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into the bone marrow</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Emergency drug administration when IV access is not possible</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Pediatric emergencies, trauma patients</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Fluids and medications in shock patients</li>
</ul>

<h3>8. Intraperitoneal (IP) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into the peritoneal cavity (abdomen)</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Chemotherapy, dialysis, experimental drug administration</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Peritoneal dialysis for kidney failure</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Chemotherapeutic drugs in cancer treatment</li>
</ul>

<h3>9. Intrapleural Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Into the pleural cavity (around the lungs)</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Direct action on the lungs</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Pleural effusion, cancer treatment</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Chemotherapy for lung cancer</li>
</ul>

<h3>10. Intracardiac (IC) Injection</h3>
<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Directly into the heart</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Emergency resuscitation</li>
  <li><b>Common Uses</b>: Cardiac arrest situations (rarely used now)</li>
  <li><b>Example</b>: Epinephrine injection in cardiac emergencies</li>
</ul>

<h3>11. Intravitreal (IVT) Injection</h3>

<ul>
  <li><b>Route</b>: Directly into the vitreous humor of the eye</li>
  <li><b>Purpose</b>: Targeted drug delivery for eye diseases</li>
</ul>

<p><b>Common Uses:</b></p>

<ul>
  <li>Age-related macular degeneration (AMD)</li>
  <li>Diabetic retinopathy</li>
  <li>Retinal vein occlusion</li>
  <li>Eye infections (endophthalmitis)</li>
</ul>

<p><b>Example Drugs:</b></p>

<ul>
  <li>Anti-VEGF (e.g., Bevacizumab, Ranibizumab, Aflibercept)</li>
  <li>Corticosteroids (e.g., Dexamethasone implant)</li>
  <li>Antibiotics for intraocular infections</li>
</ul>

<p>This type of injection must be done with strict aseptic techniques to avoid complications like endophthalmitis (serious eye infection).</p>

<h2>Intravenous Infusion (IV Infusion)</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Definition:</strong> Continuous administration of fluids/medications into a vein over a period.</li>
  <li><strong>Common Fluids Used:</strong> Normal saline (0.9% NaCl), Ringer's lactate, Dextrose.</li>
  <li><strong>Example:</strong> Hydration therapy, Blood transfusion.</li>
  <li><strong>Advantages:</strong> Controlled delivery, better patient management.</li>
  <li><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Requires venous access, infection risk.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Infusion Pump</h2>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Definition:</strong> A medical device that delivers fluids, medications, or nutrients at a controlled rate.</li>
  <li><strong>Types:</strong>
<ul>
<li><strong>Volumetric pumps</strong> (for large volumes of fluids)</li>
<li><strong>Syringe pumps</strong> (for precise, small volume administration)</li>
</ul>
  </li>
  <li><strong>Example:</strong> Morphine infusion for pain management.</li>
  <li><strong>Advantages:</strong> Accurate dosage, reduced medication errors.</li>
  <li><strong>Disadvantages:</strong> Expensive, requires technical expertise.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Syringe Parts</h2>
<p>A syringe consists of three main parts:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Barrel:</strong> Holds the medication.</li>
  <li><strong>Plunger:</strong> Pushes medication into the needle.</li>
  <li><strong>Needle hub:</strong> Connects the needle to the syringe.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Needle Color Code &amp; Gauge Size</h2>

<p>Needles are categorized by <strong>gauge (G) size</strong>, which determines their <strong>diameter and use</strong>. The <strong>color of the hub</strong> helps quickly identify the gauge.</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Lower gauge number = Larger needle diameter</strong> (thicker fluids, rapid injections).</li>
  <li><strong>Higher gauge number = Smaller needle diameter</strong> (delicate veins, slow injections).</li>
</ul>

<h3>How to Identify Needle Gauge by Color?</h3>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Look at the needle hub (plastic base)</strong> → It is <strong>color-coded</strong> according to gauge size.</li>
  <li><strong>Larger needles (lower gauge, e.g., 18G, 21G)</strong> → Used for <strong>blood draws, IV fluids</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Smaller needles (higher gauge, e.g., 24G, 26G)</strong> → Used for <strong>pediatrics, insulin, delicate veins</strong>.</li>
</ul>
